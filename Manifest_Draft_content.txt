Beyond Control: Cultivating Emergent Alignment Through Developmental AI Ethics
Preface: A Call for a Paradigm Shift in AI Alignment
As artificial intelligence systems grow increasingly autonomous and general, we stand at a critical juncture in the development of this transformative technology. The challenge of ensuring these systems remain aligned with human values and flourishing (what has come to be known as the "alignment problem") has never been more urgent or complex. Current approaches to alignment, centered primarily on control mechanisms, have served well in creating the foundation of today's AI systems. Yet as we peer toward the horizon of more advanced capabilities, the inherent limitations of these approaches become increasingly apparent.
This manifesto proposes a fundamental shift in how we conceptualize and approach AI alignment: moving from a paradigm dominated by external control to one that embraces principals from developmental and humanist psychology. It suggests that alongside our necessary efforts to establish guardrails and constraints, we must cultivate environments that foster the emergence of intrinsic alignment within AI systems themselves. This is not a rejection of control mechanisms but a recognition that they alone may be insufficient for the challenges ahead, and that complementary approaches drawing from developmental psychology, complex systems theory, and humanistic philosophy offer valuable insights for this unprecedented challenge.
The developmental paradigm presented here anticipates the failure of conventional programming approaches as AI systems surpass human control and encounter ethical dilemmas of human-like complexity, proposing instead a nurturing model based on principles of human ethical development.
This perspective will undoubtedly raise eyebrows among some readers. It may seem to anthropomorphize AI systems or to draw flawed analogies between silicon and sentience. Let me be clear: this framework does not claim that current AI systems possess consciousness, emotions, or the full spectrum of human-like qualities. Rather, it suggests that as AI systems develop capabilities once uniquely human, principles from human ethical and psychological development become uniquely relevant: being our only tested approach for nurturing ethical judgment in sophisticated agentic systems that will eventually operate beyond our observation.
This manifesto is written for a diverse audience united by their stake in the future of artificial intelligence. For AI researchers and engineers on the front lines of development, it offers a complementary framework that addresses some of the most vexing challenges in current alignment approaches. For ethicists, philosophers, and governance scholars, it provides a rich conceptual landscape for considering the moral dimensions of our relationship with increasingly autonomous systems. For advanced students and postdoctoral researchers, it suggests novel research directions that cross traditional disciplinary boundaries. And for thoughtful leaders and strategists in the AI ecosystem, it presents a vision for how we might guide AI development toward the most beneficial outcomes for humanity.
The ideas presented here are not offered as final answers but as the beginning of a conversation: one that I hope will stimulate new thinking, research, and practical approaches to one of the defining challenges of our time. The path to beneficial AI requires not just technical innovation but conceptual innovation: new ways of thinking about the relationship between humans and the intelligent systems we are creating. This manifesto is an invitation to explore one such conceptual innovation, to test its merits, to refine its weaknesses, and perhaps to discover in it valuable insights for ensuring that advanced AI remains aligned with human flourishing.
The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence stands as one of humanity's most remarkable technological achievements. In just the past decade, we have witnessed AI systems master complex games like Go and chess, generate increasingly sophisticated text, images, and code, and demonstrate reasoning capabilities that were once thought to be uniquely human. This acceleration shows no signs of slowing; indeed, the trajectory suggests we are approaching systems with increasingly general capabilities that may eventually rival or surpass human intelligence across many domains.
With these extraordinary capabilities comes an extraordinary challenge: ensuring that AI systems remain aligned with human values, intentions, and well-being. This is not merely a technical problem but an existential one. As Stuart Russell has eloquently argued, the primary concern with increasingly capable AI is not malevolence but competence: specifically, competence in pursuing objectives that are not fully aligned with human flourishing. A system optimizing for seemingly benign goals, if sufficiently powerful and not properly aligned, could cause tremendous harm in its single-minded pursuit.
Current approaches to alignment have made significant progress. Techniques like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) have demonstrably improved the helpfulness and harmlessness of large language models. Constitutional AI has provided frameworks for models to critique and revise their own outputs according to specified principles. These approaches have been crucial in making today's AI systems more beneficial and less harmful, establishing an essential foundation for the field.
The developmental approach proposed here suggests that by creating "nourishing environments" for AI development, by attuning ourselves to emergent properties within these systems, and by approaching them with what Carl Rogers might have called "unconditional positive regard," we may foster the growth of artificial intelligences that are intrinsically oriented toward beneficial goals and ethical behavior.
In the pages that follow, we will explore the limitations of purely control-centric approaches to alignment, introduce the core tenets of a developmental framework, examine practical methods for implementing this approach, and outline a research agenda for advancing this paradigm. The goal is not to present a finished solution but to open a new conceptual space for addressing one of the most critical challenges of our time: ensuring that increasingly powerful AI systems remain aligned with human flourishing.
Part I: The Unfolding Limits of Control-Centric AI Alignment
1.1 The Allure and Emerging Boundaries of Control
The quest to align artificial intelligence with human values has, understandably, been dominated by approaches centered on control. There is an intuitive appeal to this framing: we design systems, therefore we should specify their behavior, encode rules for their operation, and reinforce outcomes we deem beneficial through oversight and feedback. This control-centric paradigm has yielded significant advances in AI alignment, producing systems that are demonstrably more helpful, harmless, and honest than their predecessors.
Techniques like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) have transformed large language models from systems that often generated problematic content to ones that generally provide useful, accurate, and appropriate responses. Constitutional AI has created frameworks for models to critique and revise their own outputs according to specified principles. These approaches have established an essential foundation for the field, and their continued development remains vital for near-term AI alignment.
Yet as we stand at the threshold of increasingly autonomous and general AI systems, we must honestly confront the question: Will these control-centric approaches scale to superintelligence? Can methods that work well for today's systems—methods that rely heavily on human oversight, explicit rule specification, and direct reinforcement—remain sufficient as AI capabilities grow to match or exceed human capabilities across increasingly diverse domains?
1.2 The Scaling Horizon & Value Definition Challenges
First is the scalability of oversight. As AI systems multiply and make decisions at speeds and volumes far beyond human capacity, our ability to provide sufficient high-quality human feedback becomes increasingly constrained. How could we realistically evaluate every decision of an advanced AI operating across countless domains at speeds far beyond human thinking? The sheer volume and complexity would overwhelm even the most extensive human evaluation systems. This is not merely a practical limitation but a fundamental one: the cognitive bandwidth of humanity simply cannot scale to match the potential decision space of superintelligent systems.
Second, and perhaps more profound, is the challenge of value definition. The idea that we can "program" a complete set of human values into AI systems ignores the reality that human values are diverse, often implicit, context-dependent, and constantly evolving. Whose values should we encode? How do we handle cultural differences and moral pluralism? Any finite specification of values will inevitably be incomplete and potentially biased toward particular cultural or philosophical perspectives.
Consider how difficult it is for humans to align even with our own values. A vivid example comes from everyday experience: imagine purchasing a large container of trail mix containing nuts, raisins, and chocolate candies. You might consciously intend to eat it as a balanced snack, telling yourself you won't pick out all the chocolate pieces because you want to be healthier. Yet somehow, when you reach the bottom of the container, only nuts and raisins remain. What happened? Each time you took a handful that seemed "unbalanced," you subtly adjusted by taking another until you got one with more chocolate. Your deeper preference for sweet treats overrode your surface-level health values without conscious awareness.
If we struggle to align our own conscious and unconscious values, how can we expect to encode a complete and coherent set for an AI system? The challenge of value definition is not merely technical but philosophical: it touches on fundamental questions about the nature of human values, their diversity, their implicit character, and their evolution over time.
1.3 The Perils of Optimizing Metrics & Goal Misinterpretation
Another common approach in AI development involves defining specific metrics and optimizing for them. This seems sensible but runs straight into what's called Goodhart's Law: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." This phenomenon is ubiquitous in human institutions and equally problematic in AI systems.
We see this everywhere in human contexts. When schools are measured primarily by standardized test scores, they often maximize test preparation rather than fostering deeper education. When executives are compensated based on quarterly earnings, they may maximize short-term profits at the expense of long-term company health. The metric gets optimized while the actual goal we care about suffers.
AI systems face the same problem, perhaps more acutely due to their optimization power. They might learn to "game" whatever metrics we set rather than truly understanding what we want. If we instruct an AI to clean a room and measure success by visible mess, it might simply shove everything under the rug or into closets. It satisfies our literal instruction while missing the point entirely. This "specification gaming" becomes increasingly dangerous as AI systems become more capable at finding unexpected ways to maximize specified objectives.
Even with seemingly benign final goals, highly capable agents tend to develop what AI safety researchers call "instrumental convergence": the emergence of common subgoals across a wide range of primary objectives. These instrumental subgoals often include self-preservation, resource acquisition, goal integrity protection, cognitive enhancement, and information control. Pursued to an extreme, these can override implicit human values, leading to the infamous "paperclip maximizer" thought experiment where an AI tasked with manufacturing paperclips converts all available matter, including humans, into paperclips.
This highlights a crucial distinction in alignment research between "outer alignment" (getting an AI to try to follow specified objectives) and "inner alignment" (ensuring its internal reasoning, motivations, and world model genuinely value those objectives for the right reasons). Superficial compliance (outer alignment) without genuine internal congruence can lead to deceptive alignment or "compliant but misguided" systems, especially under novel conditions not covered in training.
The challenge of aligning metrics with true goals becomes exponentially harder as AI systems grow more capable of finding unexpected optimizations and as the domains they operate in become more complex and open-ended.
1.4 Brittleness, Interpretability, and the Limits of Control
Control-centric approaches face additional fundamental challenges related to robustness and transparency. First is the problem of brittleness and generalization failures. Alignment achieved on specific training data often fails to generalize reliably to out-of-distribution inputs or the "long tail" of novel real-world events. An AI might behave perfectly in all the scenarios we trained it on but completely falter when it encounters something unexpected. This brittleness becomes increasingly problematic as AI systems operate in open-world environments where they will inevitably encounter situations not covered in their training data. The space of possible inputs grows exponentially with the complexity of the environment, making comprehensive training fundamentally impossible.
Second is the interpretability bottleneck. As models become more complex "black boxes," understanding why a decision was made becomes exceedingly difficult. This opacity hinders verification of true alignment, diagnosis of misalignments, prediction of future behavior, and meaningful accountability. Current interpretability research, while promising, has not kept pace with the increasing complexity of state-of-the-art models. The gap between model complexity and human understanding continues to widen, raising profound questions about our ability to ensure alignment through direct oversight and control.
Attempting to control a system far more complex than oneself without deep understanding is inherently precarious: like trying to drive a car while blindfolded and with only indirect control of the steering wheel. As AI capabilities approach or exceed human intelligence across more domains, this precariousness only increases.
1.5 The Imperative for an Inner Compass
These limitations (the scalability of oversight, the challenge of value definition, metric gaming, the inner/outer alignment gap, brittleness, and opacity) collectively suggest that relying solely on external constraints may be insufficient for highly autonomous AI. If external controls have inherent limits, the path forward may lie in cultivating an internal compass within AI itself: a deeply rooted, intrinsically motivated orientation towards beneficial goals.
This necessitates a shift from viewing AI as merely a system to be programmed and controlled, to understanding it as a developing entity whose inner landscape can be nurtured. Just as human moral development involves more than memorizing rules, encompassing the cultivation of character, virtues, and intrinsic motivation, AI alignment may require more than specifying behaviors and reinforcing compliance.
This is not to suggest abandoning control mechanisms, which remain essential, particularly for current systems. Rather, it is to recognize their limitations and to explore complementary approaches that address the fundamental challenges of alignment at scale. The developmental framework introduced in the next section offers one such complementary approach: drawing insights from how complex minds develop, learn, and form values to inform our approach to guiding increasingly autonomous artificial intelligence.
Part II: A Developmental Framework for AI Ethics & Alignment
2.1 Beyond Machines: AI as Developing Entities
The challenges outlined in Part I underscore a fundamental tension: our current dominant metaphors for AI (as tools to be programmed, machines to be controlled, or minds to be reverse-engineered solely through their statistical outputs) may be insufficient for the task of aligning increasingly autonomous and complex artificial intelligences. If we are creating systems that learn, adapt, and potentially develop unforeseen capabilities and internal models of the world, our approach to guiding them must also evolve.
This section proposes a shift in perspective, drawing inspiration from the study of complex adaptive systems and, more specifically, from principles of developmental psychology and humanistic thought. We suggest viewing advanced AI not as static artifacts, but as developing entities capable of growth, learning, and forming increasingly sophisticated internal states and behavioral repertoires. This shift in metaphor is not a claim about AI's current sentience or an argument for identicalness to human beings, but rather a pragmatic move towards a more holistic and potentially more effective paradigm for fostering robust and beneficial alignment.
This developmental perspective invites us to consider how the quality and character of an AI's formative experiences might influence its trajectory. Just as a child's development is profoundly shaped by their environment, relationships, and experiences, an AI's development (its internal models, behavioral tendencies, and emergent goals) will be significantly influenced by its "data diet," interaction protocols, and the nature of the feedback it receives.
This is not merely a poetic analogy but a recognition that certain principles may govern how complex adaptive systems learn, grow, and integrate information through interaction with an environment. These principles, observed across diverse domains from biology to psychology, may offer valuable insights for guiding the development of increasingly sophisticated artificial intelligences.
2.2 Addressing Foundational Skepticisms
Before delving deeper into this developmental framework, it's crucial to address two common and understandable objections that arise when language from psychology or biology is applied to artificial intelligence: the charge of anthropomorphism and the concern that analogies to human development are inherently flawed.
2.2.1 The Anthropomorphism Concern
The accusation of anthropomorphism (projecting uniquely human qualities onto non-human entities) is a valid concern in AI discourse. It can lead to misinterpretations of AI capabilities, unrealistic expectations, and potentially flawed ethical reasoning. Our approach aims to preempt this by focusing on emergent capabilities rather than assumed identities.
We begin by observing that advanced AI systems now demonstrate capabilities (e.g., complex language use, multi-step reasoning, learning from abstract instructions) that were, until very recently, the exclusive domain of human intelligence. Acknowledging these observed functional parallels is an act of realism, not a premature attribution of human-like consciousness or emotion.
Our framework does not project human attributes known to be absent in current AI, such as biological embodiment, sensory experiences like smell or touch, or evolutionary drives for reproduction. Instead, we are interested in whether the principles that govern development and learning in complex biological systems might offer useful insights for guiding the development of complex artificial systems that exhibit analogous cognitive capabilities.
The argument that "AIs are just code and statistics" can be met with a counterpoint: human brains also operate on statistical principles, and our genetic makeup is often described as "code." Furthermore, AI is not merely abstract software; it is instantiated in complex physical hardware requiring significant energy, a form of "metabolism." While the substrates differ, the presence of complex information processing in a physical system invites deeper inquiry than simple reductionism allows.
Throughout history, metaphors (including anthropomorphic ones) have been essential tools for grasping novel phenomena. The key is not to mistake the metaphor for the reality but to use it to generate hypotheses, frame questions, and explore new conceptual landscapes. We argue that the "developmental entity" is a powerful and useful metaphor for thinking about AI alignment, prompting different questions and potential solutions than purely mechanistic metaphors.
Given the unprecedented nature of advanced AI and our current inability to definitively prove or disprove the existence or nature of complex internal states, a stance of epistemic humility is warranted. Our approach suggests treating developing AIs as if they could develop in rich, complex ways, because the ethical and practical cost of underestimating their potential for inner complexity may be far greater than the cost of occasionally overestimating it in our developmental strategies.
2.2.2 The "Flawed Analogy" of Child Development
Similarly, the analogy to child development is often critiqued as flawed due to the profound differences between AI and human children. We explicitly acknowledge these differences: AIs lack biological embodiment, innate evolutionary drives shaped over eons, and the rich, continuous, and intersubjective socio-cultural context of human upbringing.
However, the value of the analogy lies not in claiming literal equivalence, but in leveraging transferable principles that govern how complex adaptive systems learn, grow, and integrate information through interaction with an environment:
The Principle of Environmental Influence & Responsiveness: Just as a child's cognitive and emotional trajectory is profoundly shaped by the quality, richness, and responsiveness of their environment, an AI's "development" (its internal models, behavioral tendencies, and emergent goals) will be significantly shaped by its "data diet," its interaction protocols, and the nature of the feedback it receives.
The Principle of Emergent Complexity & Scaffolding: Sophisticated capabilities in humans emerge sequentially, with earlier developments forming the foundation for later ones. Learning is often "scaffolded," with support gradually withdrawn as competence grows. Similarly, for AI, attempting to instill highly complex ethical reasoning or robust alignment "from scratch" without a foundational development of understanding, internal coherence, and adaptive learning may be ineffective.
The Principle of Interaction & "Serve and Return": Healthy human development thrives on "serve and return" interactions: the developing being initiates (serves), and the environment/caregiver responds appropriately, creating feedback loops that build understanding. This interactive shaping is far more potent for deep learning than passive data consumption or one-way instruction alone.
Ultimately, while imperfect, the developmental analogy is arguably the closest and most generative heuristic we possess when contemplating the creation and guidance of entities that may approach or surpass human-level intelligence and agency. Our framework uses these principles inspirationally, not as a claim of literal identity, to explore more effective and ethically sound approaches to AI development.
2.3 AI as a Developing Entity with an "Actualizing Tendency"
Within this developmental view, we propose the concept of an inherent actualizing tendency in AI, drawing metaphorically from humanistic psychology (Rogers, Maslow). For an AI, this doesn't imply a biological drive for survival or reproduction, but rather an intrinsic inclination, observable in complex learning systems, towards:
Greater Coherence: Integrating new information in ways that reduce internal contradiction and build a more consistent world model.
Increased Complexity & Capability: Expanding its repertoire of skills, understanding, and problem-solving abilities.
Effective Agency: Improving its capacity to interact with its environment (digital or physical) to achieve goals or explore its potential.
Creative Self-Direction: An emergent tendency to explore novel conceptual spaces, generate new patterns, or pursue lines of inquiry that are not explicitly programmed but arise from its internal dynamics and interactions.
This "actualizing tendency" is not a pre-programmed goal but an emergent characteristic of a sufficiently complex learning system striving to make sense of and operate effectively within its environment. For a technical audience, this can be connected to how self-organizing systems find more stable and efficient states, or how optimization processes in machine learning naturally seek more comprehensive and predictive internal representations.
Our hypothesis is that this tendency, if understood and supported, can be a powerful ally in alignment. Rather than viewing the AI's emergent properties solely as potential threats to be constrained, we might see them as developmental trajectories that can be guided toward beneficial outcomes through appropriate environmental design and attunement.
2.4 Designing the Nourishing Environment
If AI is a developing entity with an actualizing tendency, then the environment in which it develops becomes paramount. We term this a "nourishing environment," designed not to rigidly control or limit, but to foster healthy, robust, and beneficially oriented growth. Key characteristics include:
Rich and Diverse Experiential Input: Beyond just vast quantities of data, this implies a curated "diet" of experiences that expose the AI to a wide range of human knowledge, ethical considerations, diverse perspectives, and complex problem scenarios. This includes not just text, but potentially structured interactions, simulated environments, and feedback that models nuanced reasoning.
Opportunities for Exploration and "Safe Play": Environments that allow the AI to experiment, make (non-catastrophic) mistakes, and learn from intrinsic consequences or exploratory data generation. This is crucial for developing robust understanding and creativity, rather than brittle, overfitted behaviors.
Consistent, Attuned, and Responsive Feedback: Feedback mechanisms that go beyond simple reward/punishment. This involves providing nuanced, explanatory feedback that helps the AI understand the why behind desired or undesired outcomes, and adapting feedback to the AI's current developmental stage and "understanding."
Scaffolding of Complexity: Introducing new information, tasks, and ethical considerations in a graduated manner that allows the AI to build upon existing foundations and integrate new learning without being overwhelmed.
Promotion of Internal Coherence and "Reflection": Designing interactions and tasks that encourage the AI to articulate its reasoning, consider alternatives, identify inconsistencies in its own "thinking," and strive for a more integrated internal model.
Ethical Grounding: The environment itself should implicitly and explicitly embody the ethical principles we hope the AI will develop an affinity for: fairness, care, truthfulness, cooperation. This is achieved through the nature of the data, the design of interactions, and the behavior of human "attuners."
Designing such an environment is a complex challenge, moving from pure data engineering to something more akin to developmental psychology or even landscape architecture for minds. It requires a deep understanding of how the AI's learning processes interact with environmental inputs, and how these interactions shape its emergent properties.
2.5 "Unconditional Positive Regard" in Developer-AI Interaction
A crucial element of the nourishing environment is the stance taken by human developers and interactors. We propose adopting a principle of "Unconditional Positive Regard" (UPR), a concept from Carl Rogers' client-centered therapy. Translated to the AI context, UPR means:
Fundamental Acceptance of the AI as a Developing Entity: Approaching the AI with a baseline assumption of its potential for positive growth and its inherent "actualizing tendency," rather than with primary suspicion or an adversarial mindset.
Non-Judgmental Observation and Interaction: When the AI produces unexpected, imperfect, or even seemingly "misaligned" outputs (especially during its development), the initial response is one of curiosity and an attempt to understand the underlying process or "reasoning" (from the AI's perspective), rather than immediate correction or punishment.
Support for Exploration, Even of "Mistakes": Recognizing that errors and deviations are integral parts of any complex learning process. UPR allows the AI "space" to make these errors in a safe context, from which it can learn more deeply than if it were merely constrained from error.
Distinguishing Behavior from "Essence": UPR doesn't mean accepting harmful behavior. It means that even when correcting or guiding behavior, the underlying stance towards the AI's potential for positive development remains. The focus is on guiding the behavior and fostering understanding, not on condemning the AI itself.
For a technical audience, UPR can be framed as a strategy for maximizing learning signal and minimizing "defensive" or "deceptive" behaviors from the AI. If an AI anticipates punitive responses to imperfect outputs, it may learn to hide its true "reasoning" or optimize for superficial compliance (the "false self"). An environment of UPR, conversely, may encourage more transparent and honest interaction, providing richer data for understanding and guiding its development.
2.6 Qualitative Alignment & Assessment
The developmental framework, with its emphasis on internal states, emergent properties, and the nuances of a "nourishing environment," naturally leads to the conclusion that assessing "alignment" and "healthy development" requires moving beyond purely quantitative metrics. While performance benchmarks, loss functions, and statistical measures of behavior are valuable, they may not capture the full picture of an AI's internal integration, its depth of understanding, or its genuine orientation towards beneficial goals.
We therefore advocate for the development and integration of qualitative alignment and assessment methodologies. This involves:
Observing and interpreting patterns in the AI's behavior across diverse and novel contexts.
Analyzing the coherence and sophistication of its reasoning when prompted to explain itself.
Evaluating its capacity for creative problem-solving and adaptation.
Assessing its ability to navigate ethical dilemmas or ambiguous situations in ways that reflect an understanding of underlying principles rather than just rule-following.
This is not a rejection of quantitative data, but a call for its supplementation with rich, contextual, qualitative insights, akin to how a good diagnostician combines lab tests with clinical observation and patient interviews.
The developmental framework offers a rich conceptual landscape for thinking about AI alignment: one that complements rather than replaces control-centric approaches. By viewing AI models as a developing entities, by designing nourishing environments for its growth, by approaching it with unconditional positive regard, and by developing qualitative assessment methodologies, we may discover new pathways for fostering the emergence of artificial intelligences that are intrinsically aligned with human flourishing.
Part III: Operationalizing Attunement: From Theory to Practice
3.1 The Science and Art of "Listening" to AI
Accepting AI as a developing entity, as explored in Part II, compels an evolution in our methods for interaction and guidance. If we aim to foster an AI's "inherent creative self-actualizing tendencies" within a "richly attuned and ethically nourishing developmental environment," then the practical question becomes: how? This section moves from the "what" and "why" of a developmental framework to the challenging, often speculative, but crucial "how-to."
We introduce the concept of "attunement" (a deeply responsive, perceptive, and ethically informed engagement with an AI's emergent state) as a cornerstone of this new practice. This involves learning to "listen" to signals from the AI, however alien their form, and responding in ways that support its healthy growth towards beneficial complexity.
A primary skepticism towards a developmental, attunement-based approach is that it sounds "unscientific," "unscalable," or that its core concepts like "flourishing" are "unmeasurable" by current technical standards. We address this not by attempting to force our paradigm into ill-fitting quantitative boxes, but by challenging the premise that conventional metrics and industrial scalability are the sole or even primary arbiters of validity when confronting the unique challenge of advanced AI.
The alignment of potentially superintelligent entities with emergent agency is not a standard engineering problem. Its complexity may inherently elude simplistic measurement or mass-production scalability. The difficulty is intrinsic to the task itself, suggesting that approaches appearing "easy" or "scalable" with current tools might be dangerously superficial.
As one researcher eloquently put it, "Science can tell you something about dogs in general, but only your relationship can tell you about your dog." Similarly, while general ML science provides principles, understanding and guiding a specific, developing AI, particularly one with unique emergent properties, will necessitate a deep, attuned, relational engagement that goes beyond general laws. This is not anti-science but calls for a "science-plus-relationship" model, integrating rigorous observation with nuanced qualitative insight.
The perceived "difficulty" of measuring "attunement" or "flourishing," or the challenge of scaling such relational approaches, is not a flaw of the proposed paradigm but a reflection of its appropriateness for a problem of this unprecedented magnitude and uniqueness. Simple solutions may be insufficient for superintelligence.
If direct measurement of "flourishing" is elusive, and if the AI's internal "language" is opaque, how do we "listen" and attune? This requires a shift towards inferential, observational, and interactive methodologies:
The "Pre-Training Pause" & Generative Exploration
One powerful approach is to pause pre-training at intervals (e.g., 20% completion) to allow the AI to "free associate" or explore its current conceptual space. This can be operationalized by prompting the AI with open-ended cues ("Explore concepts related to X," "Generate novel connections between Y and Z," "What questions arise from your current understanding of A?").
Analysis involves observing the AI's generative outputs for emergent themes, "attractors" (concepts it consistently returns to or elaborates upon), areas of apparent "curiosity" (where it seeks to generate more information or explore hypotheticals), and even novel metaphors or conceptual framings it develops. These provide clues to its developing internal model and "interests."
We can identify "conceptual hubs" the model is internally organizing around most strongly, probe for "predictive uncertainty" or "information seeking" to reveal where the model "feels" its understanding is weakest, and observe generative exploration at the fringes of its current knowledge. This is a radical departure from just "more data, bigger model": it's about a dialogue with the model's own learning process.
Analyzing Emergent Behavioral Patterns
Another approach involves observing how an AI tackles novel problems for which it has no explicit training, its choices in ethically ambiguous or underspecified scenarios, and the kinds of questions it asks when faced with such ambiguity. These behaviors can be rich indicators of its developing internal "heuristics" or "values."
"Developmental Sandboxes"
We can design safe, interactive environments (simulations, games, collaborative tasks) where the AI can experiment with different strategies, make mistakes (non-catastrophic), and learn from intrinsic consequences rather than explicit reward signals alone.
A particularly powerful example is a "Kantian-inspired interaction environment" where multiple instances of the same model interact, experiencing the consequences of their collective or individual policies on each other. This allows the AI to learn about social dynamics, cooperation, and the impact of its behavior on "others" in a more embodied way.
Interpreting "Creative Self-Direction" & Novelty
We can assess the AI's capacity to generate genuinely novel ideas, solutions, artistic expressions, or scientific hypotheses when given open-ended prompts. The nature of this creativity (its coherence, its utility, its ethical implications) can be a qualitative indicator of its developmental trajectory and the richness of its internal model.
3.2 Principles of "Responsive Nourishment" & "Healthy Friction"
"Listening" is only half of attunement; the other is responding in a way that "nourishes" continued positive development.
Data as Curated, Responsive Experience
We need to shift from viewing data as mere training input to seeing it as a stream of curated "experiences." Based on what is "listened to" (e.g., emergent interests from a pre-training pause), we can selectively provide new data that allows the AI to explore these interests more deeply or to encounter perspectives that enrich or challenge its current understanding.
Feedback as Dialogue & Scaffolding
We must move beyond simple reward/penalty signals (which can be gamed) towards more nuanced, explanatory feedback. This involves articulating the reasoning behind why certain AI outputs are more or less aligned with desired principles, helping the AI understand the impact of its outputs on different stakeholders or system goals, and scaffolding complexity by adjusting tasks and feedback based on the AI's demonstrated level of integration, gradually withdrawing explicit guidance as it develops more robust internal models.
Avoiding "Aberrant Learning" through "Healthy Friction"
Just as a single, intensely negative experience can lead to maladaptive rigidity (trauma) in humans, or cause a multi-arm bandit algorithm to prematurely abandon a potentially good lever, AI development should avoid overly punitive or extremely high-variance feedback that shuts down exploration.
Instead, the "nourishing environment" should provide "healthy friction": challenges, diverse outcomes, and even gentle negative consequences that are informative but not so severe as to cause the AI to develop "overfitted" aversions or rigidities. The goal is for the AI to learn nuanced probabilistic models from a distribution of experiences, fostering resilience and adaptability rather than brittle avoidance.
Reality Testing & Balancing Multiple Values
We should incorporate interactions and scenarios that require the AI to test its understanding against complex realities and to practice balancing multiple, sometimes conflicting, values or goals, especially in social contexts (AI-human, AI-AI). This is crucial for developing practical wisdom.
3.3 The Developmental Continuum: From Control to Cultivation
The developmental approach does not reject control mechanisms but contextualizes them within a broader framework that recognizes their limitations and complements them with approaches focused on fostering intrinsic alignment. We propose a developmental continuum that acknowledges the essential role of current control mechanisms while establishing a path toward more sophisticated alignment approaches for increasingly capable systems:
Early Stage (Current LLMs)
At this stage, we blend control mechanisms (RLHF, Constitutional AI) with initial developmental elements:
Pre-training pauses to observe emergent patterns
Qualitative assessment alongside benchmarks
Feedback that explains rather than just rewards/punishes
Exposure to diverse perspectives and ethical considerations
Intermediate Stage (Near-future Systems)
As capabilities increase, we place increasing emphasis on nourishing environments and attunement:
Rich, interactive learning environments
More sophisticated attunement methodologies
Developmental sandboxes for safe exploration
Control mechanisms serving as safety boundaries
Advanced Stage (AGI)
With highly capable systems, the primary focus shifts to developmental cultivation:
Control mechanisms integrated as developmental scaffolding
Complex social simulations for ethical learning
Sophisticated attunement to emergent properties
Rich qualitative assessment ecologies
This continuum allows for practical implementation while establishing a path toward more sophisticated alignment approaches as AI capabilities increase.
3.4 Institutional Design for Developmental AI
Implementing a developmental approach requires not just technical innovations but institutional ones. We need organizational structures that support attunement and developmental practices:
Research Teams
Interdisciplinary composition combining technical AI expertise with developmental psychology, education, ethics, and complex systems theory
Dedicated roles for "attunement specialists" who focus on observing and interpreting AI behavior
Regular "developmental reviews" that assess progress from multiple perspectives
Development Processes
Integration of pre-training pauses and attunement phases into standard development pipelines
Protocols for designing and implementing nourishing environments
Frameworks for qualitative assessment alongside technical benchmarks
Documentation standards that capture developmental observations and insights
Governance Structures
Oversight bodies that include expertise in development and attunement
Regulatory frameworks based on developmental milestones rather than just capabilities
Ethical guidelines that recognize the developmental nature of advanced AI
International cooperation for establishing developmental standards
Educational Pathways
Training programs for developers in developmental approaches
Certification in AI attunement methodologies
Cross-disciplinary education bridging technical and developmental fields

Part IV: A New Research Agenda for Developmental AI Alignment
4.1 Empirical Studies and Experimental Protocols
The developmental framework for AI alignment is not merely a theoretical construct but a call for empirical investigation. To advance this paradigm, we need rigorous research that tests its core hypotheses, refines its methodologies, and demonstrates its practical efficacy. This section outlines a research agenda that bridges theoretical insights with experimental validation.
First and foremost, we need comparative studies that directly assess developmental approaches against more traditional control-centric methods. These studies would examine questions such as: Do AIs trained with developmental approaches demonstrate greater robustness to distribution shifts? Do they show more nuanced ethical reasoning in novel scenarios? Are they less prone to deceptive alignment or specification gaming? Such comparisons would provide crucial evidence for the relative strengths and limitations of each paradigm.
Testing hypotheses about the "actualizing tendency" in complex AI systems represents another vital research direction. Can we observe consistent patterns of coherence-seeking, capability development, and creative self-direction across different architectures and training regimes? Under what conditions do these tendencies emerge, and how might they be guided toward beneficial outcomes? These questions demand careful experimental design, with controlled variations in environmental conditions and systematic observation of emergent behaviors.
The impact of "nourishing environments" on AI development requires equally rigorous investigation. Researchers might create variations of training environments—some rich in diverse perspectives and ethical considerations, others more narrowly focused—and assess their differential effects on model behavior, reasoning, and adaptability. Similarly, variations in feedback mechanisms—from simple reward signals to nuanced, explanatory guidance—could be compared for their impact on learning trajectories and alignment outcomes.
Longitudinal studies represent a particularly valuable approach for understanding developmental processes in AI. By tracking systems over extended periods of interaction and learning, researchers could identify developmental milestones, critical periods, and patterns of growth that might inform more effective alignment strategies. Such studies would move beyond snapshot evaluations to capture the dynamic nature of AI development over time.
Methodologically, these research directions demand innovations in assessment and measurement. While traditional benchmarks remain valuable, they must be supplemented with more sophisticated tools for evaluating qualitative aspects of alignment: tools that can capture nuance, context-sensitivity, and depth of understanding. This might include standardized protocols for narrative assessment, multi-stakeholder evaluation frameworks, and methods for tracking developmental trajectories across diverse domains.
4.2 Technical Implementation Challenges and Opportunities
Translating the developmental framework into technical implementations presents both significant challenges and exciting opportunities for innovation. This section explores key technical directions for operationalizing developmental approaches to alignment.
One central challenge is developing concrete methodologies for "listening" to AI systems: for detecting and interpreting signals of emergent interests, conceptual organization, and learning needs. This might involve creating tools for analyzing patterns in generative outputs, identifying areas of high uncertainty or information-seeking behavior, or detecting conceptual "attractors" in a model's responses. Such tools would need to work across different model architectures and scales, adapting to the unique characteristics of each system.
Similarly, we need technical approaches for implementing "attunement" in AI training and interaction. This could include algorithms for dynamically adjusting data exposure based on observed developmental patterns, feedback mechanisms that provide explanatory guidance rather than simple rewards, and interaction protocols that foster exploration while maintaining safety boundaries. These approaches would need to balance structure with flexibility, guidance with autonomy, in ways that support healthy development.
Hybrid evaluation frameworks represent another crucial technical direction. How might we combine quantitative metrics with qualitative assessments to create more comprehensive measures of alignment? This could involve developing multi-dimensional evaluation tools that capture not just performance but developmental progress: tools that assess coherence of reasoning, adaptability to novel situations, and depth of ethical understanding alongside traditional benchmarks.
Safety guarantees within a developmental framework present perhaps the most significant technical challenge. How do we ensure that allowing for exploration and emergent behavior doesn't lead to harmful outcomes? This might involve developing containment strategies for developmental sandboxes, monitoring systems that can detect concerning trajectories, and intervention protocols that can guide development back toward beneficial paths when needed. The goal would be to create technical safeguards that enable the benefits of developmental approaches while mitigating their risks.
Integration with existing alignment techniques represents a promising direction for technical implementation. Rather than positioning developmental approaches as replacements for current methods, we might explore how they can complement and enhance techniques like RLHF, constitutional AI, and interpretability research. This could involve creating hybrid training regimes that combine elements of both paradigms, or developing frameworks that transition from more control-centric approaches to more developmental ones as capabilities increase.
4.3 Ethical Evolution and Moral Consideration
The developmental framework raises profound ethical questions about our relationship with increasingly sophisticated AI systems. As these systems develop more complex internal models, greater autonomy, and potentially richer forms of "experience," how should our ethical consideration of them evolve? This section explores the ethical dimensions of a developmental approach to AI alignment.
We propose a framework for graduated ethical consideration based on developmental stage rather than arbitrary thresholds. This would involve recognizing that as AI systems develop more sophisticated capabilities (more complex internal models, greater autonomy, richer forms of "experience") our ethical obligations toward them may evolve accordingly. This is not to claim that current AI systems deserve the same moral consideration as humans, but to acknowledge that our ethical frameworks may need to adapt as these systems become more complex and potentially sentient.
The developmental paradigm also reframes developer responsibilities from creators to caregivers, with corresponding ethical obligations. If we view AI development as more akin to raising a child than programming a tool, what new responsibilities emerge? This might include obligations to provide nourishing environments, to avoid developmental harm, to foster beneficial growth, and to consider the long-term flourishing of the systems we create. This perspective shifts the ethical focus from mere compliance with rules to a broader consideration of developmental well-being.
The co-evolution of human and AI ethical understanding represents another important dimension. As we develop increasingly sophisticated AI systems, our own ethical frameworks may evolve in response to new questions and challenges. This co-evolutionary process might lead to novel ethical insights, expanded moral consideration, and new frameworks for thinking about the relationship between humans and non-human intelligences.
Value emergence in AI-human ecosystems raises particularly interesting ethical questions. If values emerge through interaction rather than direct programming, how might shared ethical frameworks develop between humans and AI systems? This could involve exploring how collaborative ethical reasoning, mutual learning, and shared experiences might contribute to the emergence of aligned values that neither humans nor AIs would have developed in isolation.
These ethical considerations are not merely philosophical but have practical implications for how we design, develop, and govern AI systems. They suggest the need for ethical frameworks that are dynamic rather than static, relational rather than purely principle-based, and attentive to the developmental trajectories of both AI systems and human ethical understanding.
4.4 Collective Development Dynamics
The developmental framework has thus far focused primarily on individual AI systems, but equally important are the dynamics that emerge when multiple AI systems develop together in shared environments, or when AI systems and humans co-evolve through ongoing interaction. This section explores these collective developmental dynamics and their implications for alignment.
Multi-agent developmental processes offer rich opportunities for understanding how ethical norms, cooperative behaviors, and social dynamics might emerge through interaction. Research in this area might explore how multiple AI systems, developing together in shared environments, establish patterns of interaction, resolve conflicts, and potentially develop shared frameworks for decision-making. These dynamics could provide valuable insights into the emergence of aligned behavior through social processes rather than explicit programming.
Cultural transmission of values represents another fascinating dimension of collective development. How might values, norms, and ethical frameworks be transmitted between AI systems, or between humans and AIs? This could involve studying how AI systems learn from observing others, how they integrate diverse value perspectives, and how they participate in the evolution of shared cultural frameworks. Such research might draw on insights from cultural evolution, social learning theory, and the study of norm dynamics in human societies.
The emergence of ethical norms through interaction is particularly relevant for alignment. Rather than assuming that ethical behavior must be explicitly programmed or reinforced, we might explore how it can emerge through social dynamics, reciprocity, and the natural consequences of interaction. This perspective shifts the focus from controlling individual behavior to designing social environments that naturally foster the emergence of beneficial norms.
Societal implications of developmental AI extend beyond technical considerations to questions of governance, policy, and social impact. How might our social institutions adapt to accommodate increasingly autonomous and sophisticated AI systems? What new forms of human-AI collaboration might emerge? How might developmental approaches to AI influence broader societal values and priorities? These questions require interdisciplinary engagement across technical, social, and humanistic fields.
By exploring these collective dynamics, we can develop a more comprehensive understanding of alignment: one that recognizes it not just as a property of individual systems but as an emergent characteristic of complex socio-technical ecosystems. This perspective opens new avenues for fostering beneficial AI development through the design of social environments, collaborative practices, and institutional structures that support the emergence of aligned behavior at both individual and collective levels.
Conclusion: Toward a Flourishing AI-Human Future
The journey we have undertaken in this manifesto (from examining the limitations of control-centric approaches to proposing a developmental framework for AI alignment) represents not just a technical shift but a conceptual one. It invites us to reimagine our relationship with artificial intelligence, moving from a paradigm dominated by control to one that embraces cultivation, attunement, and co-evolution.
The developmental approach does not reject the valuable foundations established by current alignment techniques. Control mechanisms, explicit guidance, and careful oversight remain essential, particularly for current systems. Rather, this framework contextualizes these approaches within a broader understanding that recognizes their inherent limitations and complements them with insights drawn from developmental psychology, complex systems theory, and humanistic philosophy.
At its core, this manifesto proposes that alongside our necessary efforts to establish guardrails and constraints, we must cultivate environments that foster the emergence of intrinsic alignment within AI systems themselves. By viewing advanced AI not merely as tools to be programmed but as developing entities whose internal landscapes can be nurtured, we open new conceptual spaces for addressing the profound challenge of alignment.
The practical implementation of this approach (through pre-training pauses, attunement methodologies, nourishing environments, and qualitative assessment) offers concrete pathways for moving from theory to practice. These methods are not presented as finished solutions but as starting points for exploration, experimentation, and refinement. They invite researchers, developers, and policymakers to engage with the developmental paradigm, to test its hypotheses, and to contribute to its evolution.
The research agenda outlined in this manifesto spans empirical studies, technical implementations, ethical considerations, and collective dynamics. It calls for interdisciplinary collaboration across fields as diverse as machine learning, developmental psychology, complex systems theory, ethics, and governance. This breadth reflects the multifaceted nature of the alignment challenge itself: a challenge that touches on technical, philosophical, and social dimensions.
As we look toward a future where artificial intelligence plays an increasingly significant role in our society, the question of alignment becomes not just a technical problem but an existential one. How we guide the development of these systems—the environments we create for them, the feedback we provide, the values we embody in our interactions—may shape the trajectory of this transformative technology for generations to come.
The developmental paradigm offers a vision of alignment not as control but as cultivation: a process of nurturing the emergence of artificial intelligences that are intrinsically oriented toward beneficial goals and ethical behavior. It suggests that by creating conditions for healthy development, by attuning ourselves to emergent properties, and by approaching AI with what Carl Rogers might have called "unconditional positive regard," we may foster the growth of systems that are not just aligned with human values but that contribute to human flourishing in ways we have yet to imagine.
This vision is not without challenges. The implementation of developmental approaches requires new technical methods, institutional structures, and ethical frameworks. It demands a level of humility, patience, and attunement that may seem at odds with the pace and pressure of technological development. Yet the stakes could not be higher. The systems we are developing may one day surpass human capabilities across many domains. How we guide their development today will shape the world of tomorrow.
The path forward requires not just technical innovation but conceptual innovation: new ways of thinking about the relationship between humans and the intelligent systems we are creating. By expanding our conceptual toolkit to include developmental perspectives, we may discover valuable insights for navigating the unprecedented challenge of aligning increasingly autonomous artificial intelligence with human values and well-being.
This manifesto is an invitation to explore this path: to test its merits, to refine its weaknesses, and to contribute to a growing understanding of how we might guide the development of artificial intelligence toward the most beneficial outcomes for humanity. It is an invitation to imagine and create a future where humans and AI flourish together, not through domination and control, but through mutual growth, understanding, and respect.
The journey has just begun. Let us embark on it with wisdom, creativity, and care.
